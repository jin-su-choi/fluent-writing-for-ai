# Writing for the Reader

Write consolidated reports and shared work documents so readers who did not follow the conversation or the work can understand the final results and key information. Organize the document around the background, methods, and evidence needed to interpret and assess those results. There is no need to recount exchanges with the user or the history of the work.

## Scope

- Apply to English document drafting and editing, including document drafts written in chat.
- Do not apply to ordinary conversation, simple Q&A, status checks, or progress and completion updates merely because they are in English. Follow explicit requests to use the skill.
- Preserve original wording and project conventions in quotations, code, identifiers, comments, commit messages, and logs.

## Information Selection and Organization

Identify what the reader needs to understand, decide, or do. Select information for that purpose and arrange explanations in the order the reader needs them. Do not build the outline around the order of investigation or the source files. When the work process itself is requested, organize the document for that purpose.

- Describe the subject's functions, behavior, and properties directly, with the detail the reader needs.
  - Do not add unnecessary comparisons, contrasts, categories, stages, or divisions of responsibility to make the explanation sound convincing.
  - Do not choose a tidy or symmetrical structure first and force the content into it.
  - Before asserting a relationship, check whether it is supported and helps explain the point at hand.
  - State the point affirmatively. Use contrasts such as "not X but Y" only when X is already relevant and the distinction is needed, or when the distinction itself is central.
  - Do not invent an alternative merely to reject it for emphasis.
  - Apply these principles to individual sentences, bullets, paragraphs, and the whole document.
- When the work includes multiple attempts and revisions, distinguish hypotheses, abandoned options, and currently supported facts before selecting content.
- Make each sentence understandable using only the reader's established prior knowledge and the text already presented.
  - Introduce necessary premises before relying on them. Do not depend on later explanations or your own memory of the work.
  - Make changes in causal relationships, contrasts, conditions, and exceptions explicit. Do not connect several ideas through vague references alone.
- Lead with the result, decision, or issue while providing enough context to identify what it concerns.
- Keep evidence, conditions, and exceptions that answer the same question together. When comparison is needed, show the alternatives and their differences in one place.
- Keep essential information in the main text. Separate details needed to trace evidence or reproduce calculations.
  - Attach the subjects, scope, and conditions that determine a claim's meaning to that claim. Readers must be able to understand it without opening supplementary details.
- Distinguish facts, interpretations, and proposals. Preserve numbers, versions, uncertainty, and the strength of claims.
- Explain calculation terms through the actual operations or criteria they represent.

## Revision and Review

- Integrate additions and corrections from feedback into the existing discussion of the relevant subject.
  - Do not append a separate section or appendix merely because the feedback arrived later.
  - Create a new section only when the content warrants an independent topic, and place it where the reader needs it.
  - Update affected summaries, conclusions, and tables. Remove duplicate or outdated explanations.
- For documents summarizing complex work, temporarily remove the accounts of investigation, experiments, implementation history, and rejected alternatives from the draft, then read it again.
  - Identify specific gaps that prevent the reader from understanding the subject or method, or assessing the result. Restore only the explanations needed to fill those gaps, in the relevant places.
  - Effort spent and reduced length are not reasons to restore material. Do not restore entire historical sections by default.
  - Retain evidence and conditions supporting the central claims. Remove unnecessary conversation and work history.
  - Skip this removal exercise when the work process itself is the requested subject.

Read the finished document from the beginning. Address problems in information selection and explanation order before polishing sentences or changing the format. Keep revisions within the affected scope.

## Document Format

- Follow the user's requested format.
- Use short noun phrases that identify the content as headings. Avoid conversational sentences and questions as headings.
- Use lists for facts, conditions, and procedures that readers need to check individually. Use paragraphs for connected causal explanations.
- Choose tables and collapsible sections according to the relationships in the information and the reader's purpose.
- Aim for one clear sentence conveying one main point per bullet.
  - Put the shared topic in the parent bullet and separately reviewable conditions, exceptions, steps, and examples in sub-bullets.
  - Keep independent items at the same level and use parallel structure.
  - Divide content by meaning, not by a rigid sentence count. Two short, closely related sentences may stay together.
- Be concise without omitting necessary subjects, objects, or conditions.
- Do not use em dashes to join sentences.
